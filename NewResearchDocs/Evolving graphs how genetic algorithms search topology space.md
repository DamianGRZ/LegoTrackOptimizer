# Evolving graphs: how genetic algorithms search topology space

**The central challenge of evolving network topologies with genetic algorithms is the encoding problem — how a linear genome maps to a variable-size graph while preserving locality, feasibility, and meaningful crossover.** Three decades of research across neuroevolution, circuit design, VLSI layout, and infrastructure networks have produced a rich taxonomy of solutions, from NEAT's innovation numbers to BRKGA's decoder paradigm to graph grammars that guarantee validity by construction. The evidence strongly favors indirect or construction-based encodings over flat adjacency representations for most topology problems, with the critical differentiator being *locality* — whether small genotype changes produce small phenotype changes. This report synthesizes the major approaches, their encodings, decoders, and constraint-handling mechanisms, with particular attention to how each handles multi-port components and branching structures.

## NEAT solved crossover of variable-topology graphs through historical markings

Kenneth Stanley and Risto Miikkulainen's NEAT (2002) remains the most influential approach to evolving graph topology. Its genome consists of two gene types: **node genes** (specifying neuron type and ID) and **connection genes** (specifying source node, target node, weight, enable/disable bit, and a globally unique innovation number). The genome is variable-length — it grows as structural mutations add nodes and connections.

NEAT's three interdependent mechanisms each address a fundamental problem in topology evolution. **Innovation numbers** assign a permanent historical marker to every new gene when it first appears via structural mutation. A global counter increments with each novel structural change, and identical mutations occurring independently in the same generation receive the same number. During crossover, genes from two parents are aligned by innovation number rather than position — matching genes (same innovation) are randomly inherited, while disjoint and excess genes come from the fitter parent. This alignment mechanism solves the *competing conventions problem*, where functionally identical networks can have entirely different gene orderings, making positional crossover destructive.

**Speciation** protects structural innovations that initially reduce fitness. NEAT computes a compatibility distance δ between genomes based on the count of excess genes, disjoint genes, and average weight difference of matching genes. Organisms are grouped into species when δ falls below a threshold, and fitness is shared within species — each organism's fitness is divided by its species' population. This prevents any single topology from monopolizing the population and gives novel structures time to optimize their weights before competing globally.

**Complexification** starts every individual as a minimal network — inputs directly connected to outputs with no hidden nodes — and grows structure only when beneficial. The "add node" mutation splits an existing connection by inserting a new node with weight-1 input and original-weight output, minimizing initial disruption. The "add connection" mutation creates a new edge between previously unconnected nodes. Ablation studies showed these mechanisms are deeply interdependent: removing speciation dropped success rates to 20%, removing complexification made search **8.5× slower**, and removing historical markings broke both crossover and speciation.

NEAT naturally handles multi-port and branching structures because each connection gene is independent — any node can have arbitrary fan-in and fan-out, and recurrent connections enable memory. HyperNEAT (Stanley, D'Ambrosio & Gauci, 2009) extends this with an indirect encoding where a CPPN (Compositional Pattern Producing Network) is queried with geometric coordinates to determine connection weights, enabling evolution of large-scale networks that exploit symmetry and regularity. The direct encoding's primary limitation is scalability — every connection is explicitly represented, making networks with thousands of neurons impractical without indirect extensions.

## CGP exploits non-coding genes for neutral drift across fitness plateaus

Cartesian Genetic Programming, developed by Julian Miller (1997–2000), takes a fundamentally different approach: a **fixed-length integer string** encodes a directed acyclic graph on a grid of computational nodes. Each node is encoded by a function gene (indexing into a lookup table) and *a* connection genes (addresses of input sources). Additional output genes specify which nodes provide program outputs. The total genotype length is fixed at *n_n* × (*a* + 1) + *n_o* integers regardless of the phenotype's complexity.

The genotype-to-phenotype mapping is recursive and many-to-one. Starting from output genes, the decoder traces connections backward to identify all **active** (coding) nodes. Any node not on a path from inputs to outputs is **inactive** — its genes exist in the genome but don't affect behavior. This creates CGP's defining feature: variable effective phenotype size within a fixed genotype. A genome with 500 nodes might produce a phenotype using only 12.

This redundancy enables **neutral genetic drift**, CGP's primary exploration mechanism. The standard (1+4) evolutionary strategy accepts offspring with fitness *equal to* the parent. When mutations affect only inactive genes, fitness is unchanged but the genotype shifts — potentially to a position where a single further mutation to an active gene produces a beneficial change. Turner and Miller (2015) demonstrated that this drift strongly aids escape from local optima and that *not mutating inactive genes leads to worse search*. The optimal number of nodes is often much larger than needed for the solution, with an approximately quadratic relationship between grid size and performance.

CGP's graph encoding naturally supports **fan-out** (code reuse) — multiple nodes can reference the same predecessor, creating branching structures without duplication. Multiple outputs draw from potentially different subgraphs. Unlike tree-based GP, CGP does not suffer from bloat because phenotype size is bounded by the grid. However, CGP exhibits **positional bias** (nodes near inputs are disproportionately likely to be active), and crossover is generally ineffective — the method relies almost entirely on mutation, maintaining only a single lineage at a time without population-level diversity mechanisms like NEAT's speciation.

## Grammar-based encodings generate branching structures from compact rule sets

Three grammar-based paradigms address topology evolution with increasing generality: Grammatical Evolution for string-based derivations, L-systems for branching morphologies, and graph grammars for arbitrary graph topologies.

**Grammatical Evolution** (Ryan, Collins & O'Neill, 1998) uses a variable-length integer string of codons that drive production rule selection in a BNF grammar. For each non-terminal encountered during leftmost derivation, the next codon *c* selects production rule *c* mod *r* from *r* available rules. A wrapping operator loops the reading position back to the start if codons are exhausted before all non-terminals resolve. Recursive grammar rules like `<tree> ::= node(<tree>, <tree>) | leaf` naturally generate branching structures of arbitrary depth. The approach achieves complete separation between the search engine and output language, but suffers from a **ripple effect** — a single codon change can cascade through multiple downstream production choices, destroying evolved building blocks and creating poor locality.

**L-systems** (Lindenmayer, 1968) offer a more natural encoding for branching structures. Production rules are applied *in parallel* to all symbols simultaneously, and bracket notation `[` / `]` creates multi-port branching junctions via push/pop on a turtle graphics state stack. The L-system rules themselves serve as the genotype — a few rules generate exponentially complex structures through iterative application. Hornby and Pollack (2001, 2003) demonstrated that L-system-encoded virtual creatures and modular robots achieved an **order of magnitude more complexity** than direct encodings, with natural regularity, symmetry, and modularity. Parametric L-systems extend this by associating numerical parameters with symbols, enabling simultaneous topology and geometry optimization. The primary limitation is that standard L-systems generate tree structures — cycles and meshes require significant extensions.

**Graph grammars** operate directly on graphs via production rules of the form L → R, where L is a pattern subgraph to match and R is its replacement. The Double Pushout (DPO) approach (Ehrig et al., 1973) uses an interface graph K preserved during transformation, while the Single Pushout (SPO) approach (Löwe, 1993) automatically removes dangling edges. MIT's **RoboGrammar** (Zhao et al., 2020, SIGGRAPH Asia) demonstrated that ~10 grammar rules can describe **hundreds of thousands** of fabricable robot designs, with a graph neural network-based heuristic guiding rule application. Guo et al. (2022, ICLR Oral) applied learned graph grammars to molecular generation, collapsing substructures into single nodes to discover compact rule sets. The key advantage is **guaranteed structural validity** by grammar design — any derivation sequence produces a valid structure. The key weakness is that designing the grammar requires significant domain expertise and may inadvertently exclude innovative solutions.

## VLSI encodings demonstrate the power of indirect representations for layout problems

VLSI floorplanning has produced some of the most sophisticated indirect graph encodings. The **sequence pair** (Murata et al., 1996) encodes a general floorplan as two permutations (Γ⁺, Γ⁻) of module indices. If module *a* precedes *b* in both permutations, *a* is left of *b*; if *a* precedes *b* in Γ⁺ but follows *b* in Γ⁻, *a* is above *b*. This generates horizontal and vertical constraint graphs whose longest paths determine chip dimensions, with O(n²) evaluation time. Three simple perturbation operations (rotate module, swap in one sequence, swap in both) suffice to reach any floorplan from any other. Unlike slicing tree representations, which cannot encode non-slicing patterns like the "wheel" configuration of five cyclic modules, sequence pairs represent **all** general floorplans.

The **B*-tree** (Chang et al., 2000) uses an ordered binary tree where left children represent rightward adjacency and right children represent upward adjacency, achieving O(n) evaluation via contour-based packing. **Normalized postfix expressions** (Valenzuela & Wang, 2002) serialize slicing trees as strings satisfying a balloting property, where every expression is guaranteed legal under standard crossover.

For routing, **multi-terminal nets** — nets connecting 3+ pins — are the VLSI analog of multi-port components. These require Steiner trees with branching points, and GAs attack them through Prüfer number encoding (every sequence decodes to a valid tree), edge-set encoding (union of parent edges followed by random spanning tree construction), or Hanan grid restriction (limiting Steiner points to grid intersections). The coevolutionary approach to 3D multi-pipe routing (Furuholmen et al., 2010) evolved separate populations for each pipe, outperforming monolithic encodings by exploiting natural problem decomposition.

## Bond graphs provide a unified multi-port representation across physical domains

The most principled approach to multi-port components comes from **bond graph genetic programming** (Seo, Fan, Hu, Goodman & Rosenberg, 2003). Bond graphs model energy flow across physical domains using primitive elements: one-port components (R, C, I, sources), two-port transformers (TF, GY), and critically, **multi-port junctions** — 0-junctions (equal effort, flows sum to zero) and 1-junctions (equal flow, efforts sum to zero). These junctions naturally support arbitrary fan-out and fan-in, serving as the branching/merging points in any topology.

The genotype is a GP tree of topology-manipulating operators that progressively grow a bond graph from an embryonic model. The decoder executes the tree depth-first, then performs **causality analysis** — a fast structural check that eliminates physically unrealizable designs before expensive simulation. This two-stage evaluation dramatically reduces computational cost. The approach is domain-independent: a mechanical spring-mass-damper system and an RLC electrical circuit have identical bond graph representations. Applications include eigenvalue placement, analog filters, and mixed electromechanical systems.

Koza's earlier GP-based circuit evolution (1996–1999) handled multi-terminal devices like transistors through construction functions that specify connections to 3 nodes simultaneously, growing circuits from embryonic starting points. However, Shi et al. (2022) identified that Koza's five basic instructions cannot support all possible circuit topologies — a limitation addressed by newer multi-tree representations designed to be "three-terminal devices friendly."

## The encoding taxonomy reveals locality as the decisive factor

A systematic comparison of graph encodings reveals a clear hierarchy. **Adjacency matrix** encoding (O(N²) genome, binary entries) is simple but wasteful for sparse graphs and provides no structural guarantees — crossover freely creates disconnected or invalid topologies. **Edge list** encoding is space-efficient (O(M)) but creates variable-length genome complications. Raidl and Julstrom (2003) showed that **edge-set encoding** — where crossover takes the union of parent edges and runs a random spanning tree algorithm — achieves the best combination of O(N) space, feasibility, full coverage, high locality, and high heritability.

Among tree-specific encodings, **Prüfer sequences** (N-2 integers bijectively encoding labeled trees) guarantee validity but have **critically low locality** — changing a single integer can alter many edges. Rothlauf and Goldberg (2000) formally demonstrated this deficiency. **Dandelion codes** (Thompson et al., 2007) offer bounded, asymptotically optimal locality among Cayley codes with linear-time encoding/decoding, consistently outperforming Prüfer and Blob codes. **NetKeys** (Rothlauf et al., 2002) adapt random keys to tree encoding — real-valued edge weights are sorted and fed to Kruskal's algorithm, producing spanning trees with high locality that work on sparse graphs unlike Cayley codes.

| Encoding | Genome Size | Validity | Locality | Variable Size | Multi-port Support |
|----------|-------------|----------|----------|---------------|-------------------|
| Adjacency matrix | O(N²) | Not guaranteed | Low | Poor (fixed N) | Implicit via degree |
| Edge list | O(M) | Not guaranteed | Moderate | Natural | Explicit edge typing |
| Prüfer sequence | O(N) | Guaranteed (trees) | Very low | No | Via repeated indices |
| Dandelion code | O(N) | Guaranteed (trees) | Moderate | No | Via repeated indices |
| NetKeys / BRKGA | O(M) or O(N) | Guaranteed via decoder | High | Via decoder design | Via decoder design |
| Edge-set | O(N) | Guaranteed via operator | High | Limited | Via operator design |
| NEAT construction | Variable | Guaranteed by construction | High | Natural | Natural fan-in/fan-out |
| CGP grid | Fixed O(N·a) | Guaranteed by addressing | Moderate | Effective size varies | Natural fan-out |
| Graph grammar | Rule sequence | Guaranteed by grammar | Variable | Natural | By grammar design |

The fundamental tension is between **representation simplicity** (simple genotype, standard operators, decoder-guaranteed feasibility) and **search efficiency** (direct representation, high locality, meaningful structural crossover). BRKGA exemplifies the simplicity end: every gene is a real number in [0,1], and a problem-specific decoder maps any vector to a feasible solution. The biased crossover operator inherits from the elite parent with probability ρ_e > 0.5, creating double elitism. Gonçalves and Resende (2011) demonstrated applications to OSPF routing, survivable network design, and wavelength assignment. BRKGA's weakness is that the indirect mapping can create deceptive fitness landscapes where genotypically similar chromosomes decode to very different solutions.

## Constraint handling spans a spectrum from penalties to guaranteed feasibility

Six constraint-handling paradigms appear across the literature, ordered by increasing sophistication:

- **Death penalty** rejects infeasible solutions outright — simple but wastes evaluations and loses information from near-feasible solutions
- **Static penalty functions** add weighted violation terms to fitness — f_penalized = f(x) + Σ R_j · violation_j — but penalty weights are notoriously hard to tune
- **Dynamic/adaptive penalties** increase coefficients over generations or adjust based on the fraction of feasible solutions in the population
- **Repair operators** transform infeasible solutions using domain heuristics (e.g., reconnecting disconnected components via BFS, removing cycle-creating edges) — powerful but introduce search bias and are problem-specific
- **Constraint-preserving operators** design crossover and mutation to always produce valid offspring (e.g., Raidl's edge-set crossover, NEAT's structural mutations, bridge-aware edge removal using Tarjan's algorithm)
- **Decoder-based approaches** guarantee feasibility by construction — any genotype maps to a valid phenotype through a deterministic algorithm, cleanly separating the problem-independent search framework from problem-specific constraint knowledge

For connectivity constraints specifically, spanning-tree-based decoders inherently produce connected graphs, while degree constraints can be enforced during construction by skipping edges that would exceed maximum degree. The decoder paradigm mirrors the kernel/feature-space separation in machine learning — the search operates in a smooth, unconstrained space while the decoder handles all domain complexity.

## Railway and infrastructure networks reveal a significant research gap

Railway network optimization with evolutionary algorithms is dominated by *operational* problems — timetabling, scheduling, rescheduling — on fixed infrastructure. Physical track design work focuses almost exclusively on **alignment optimization**: finding optimal horizontal and vertical profiles through terrain. Li et al. (2019) used a hybrid PSO-GA to optimize mountain railway alignments for the Sichuan-Tibet Railway, encoding horizontal and vertical points of intersection as coordinate particles, with constraints on minimum curve radii, maximum gradients, and tangent lengths per Chinese design codes. The system found solutions superior to experienced human designers.

For station track layouts, Roanes-Lozano (2022) modeled topology as directed graphs where turnouts are multi-port vertices (3-port for standard turnouts, 4-port for scissors crossings), but used enumerative graph analysis rather than evolutionary search. The literature reveals **a significant gap in direct evolutionary optimization of track junction topology** — deciding where to place turnouts, how many tracks to include, and how they interconnect.

Water distribution network optimization provides the closest analog, with the most mature GA literature. The standard approach (Savic & Walters, 1997; Dandy, Simpson & Murphy, 1996) fixes the network topology and optimizes pipe diameters from discrete commercial sizes using binary or Gray-coded chromosomes decoded through hydraulic simulation (EPANET). T-junctions and manifolds are part of the fixed topology, not evolved. Gas pipeline network topology optimization (Yang & Chen, 2025) uses binary encoding of potential connections with adaptive GA, achieving **65% improvement** over PSO for connectivity reliability in an 89-node network.

The most promising directions for railway topology optimization combine insights from multiple fields: construction-sequence encoding (from NEAT/graph grammars) for handling variable-size graphs with typed multi-port junctions, parameterized templates for station throat designs, and hierarchical decomposition separating corridor-level from station-level topology decisions.

## Conclusion: construction-based encodings dominate for topology search

The evidence across three decades of evolutionary topology optimization converges on several clear conclusions. **Flat encodings fail for topology search** — adjacency matrices create rugged landscapes and Prüfer sequences destroy locality, while edge-set and decoder-based approaches succeed precisely because they maintain the genotype-phenotype correlation that evolutionary search requires. **Construction-based and grammar-based encodings offer the strongest framework** for multi-port, variable-size topology problems: NEAT's historical markings solve crossover alignment, CGP's neutral drift enables exploration through non-coding regions, and graph grammars guarantee validity while compressing vast design spaces into small rule sets.

The most underexplored area is the evolution of typed, constrained infrastructure topologies — networks where nodes have heterogeneous port counts (turnouts, junctions, manifolds), connections carry geometric constraints (minimum radii, clearances), and the number of components is itself variable. Bond graph GP provides the most principled multi-port representation, but has not been applied to infrastructure-scale problems. The fusion of graph grammar validity guarantees with BRKGA's decoder simplicity and NEAT's structural alignment mechanisms represents a promising but unrealized synthesis. For practitioners facing a topology optimization problem today, the choice reduces to a simple heuristic: if the topology is tree-structured, use edge-set encoding with constraint-preserving crossover; if it requires variable-size graphs with cycles, use a construction-sequence approach with speciation; if rapid prototyping matters most, use BRKGA with a carefully designed decoder.
