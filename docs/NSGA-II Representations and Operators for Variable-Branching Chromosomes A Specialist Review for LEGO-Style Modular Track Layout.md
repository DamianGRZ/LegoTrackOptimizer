# NSGA-II Representations and Operators for Variable-Branching Chromosomes: A Specialist Review for LEGO-Style Modular Track Layout

## TL;DR

- For chromosomes that decode into a tree-like phenotype with an a priori unknown number of branches — your switch-spawned LEGO/4DBrix sub-loops — the dominant successful pattern in the literature is a **fixed-length, segmented integer-keyed genome with a deterministic, construction-based decoder** (BRKGA-style; Gonçalves & Resende, *J. Heuristics* 17, 487–525, 2011, doi:10.1007/s10732-010-9143-1; Londe, Pessoa, Andrade & Resende, *EJOR* 321:1–22, 2025, doi:10.1016/j.ejor.2024.03.030). True variable-length representations (Goldberg's messy GA, Kim & de Weck's VCL-GA, GP trees) are theoretically richer but practically penalised by linkage destruction and bloat, and they integrate poorly with NSGA-II's crowding-distance machinery — your current architecture is on the right side of this trade-off.
- The right operator stack for your problem is therefore: (i) **biased uniform crossover applied per chromosome region** with per-region inheritance probabilities ρ ≥ 0.5 (Gonçalves & Resende 2011; Andrade, Toso, Gonçalves & Resende, *EJOR* 289:17–30, 2021, doi:10.1016/j.ejor.2019.11.037), (ii) **a portfolio of structurally typed mutations** — one per semantic region (main-loop swap/replace, switch-mask flip, branch mutation, crossing toggle) chosen by an **adaptive operator selection scheme such as FRRMAB** (Li, Fialho, Kwong & Zhang, *IEEE TEVC* 18(1):114–130, 2014, doi:10.1109/TEVC.2013.2239648) or UCB1 (Auer et al. 2002; α-UCB in ALNS, Hendel, *Math. Prog. Comp.* 14:185–221, 2022, doi:10.1007/s12532-021-00209-7), and (iii) **NSGA-II's standard binary tournament with rank + crowding-distance selection** (Deb, Pratap, Agarwal & Meyarivan, *IEEE TEVC* 6(2):182–197, 2002, doi:10.1109/4235.996017), unmodified, since the *genotype* is fixed-length even when the *phenotype* is not.
- The 70–95 % naïve-crossover destruction rate observed in your pilot is the textbook prediction of variable-length / context-sensitive encodings (Poli, Langdon & McPhee, *A Field Guide to Genetic Programming*, 2008; O'Neill, Ryan, Keijzer & Cattolico, *GPEM* 4:67–93, 2003, doi:10.1023/A:1021877127167). The fix is not a smarter crossover on the construction sequence but **decoder-induced canonicalisation plus segment-selective biased crossover with high mutation share** — the same lesson that BRKGA, grouping GAs (Falkenauer, *J. Heuristics* 2:5–30, 1996, doi:10.1007/BF00226291), and grammatical evolution all converged on independently.

## Key Findings

**(1) Three families of representation, with very different cost/expressiveness profiles.** The literature on chromosomes for solutions whose structure is itself a decision variable splits cleanly into (a) genuinely variable-length linear genomes — the messy GA of Goldberg, Korb & Deb (*Complex Systems* 3:493–530, 1989) with its cut-and-splice operator, the variable-chromosome-length GA of Kim & de Weck (*Struct. Multidiscip. Optim.* 29:445–456, 2005, doi:10.1007/s00158-004-0498-5) for progressive topology refinement, and the variable-length sensor-scheduling GA family reviewed in MDPI *Sensors* 21:3990 (2021); (b) tree-structured genomes — Koza's genetic programming (*Genetic Programming*, MIT Press, 1992) with subtree crossover, Montana's Strongly Typed GP (*Evol. Comput.* 3(2):199–230, 1995, doi:10.1162/evco.1995.3.2.199), Koza's Automatically Defined Functions and architecture-altering operations (*Genetic Programming II*, MIT Press, 1994), and grammatical evolution (O'Neill & Ryan, *IEEE TEVC* 5(4):349–358, 2001, doi:10.1109/4235.942529); and (c) indirect/generative encodings that *grow* the phenotype from a small genome — Gruau's cellular encoding (PhD, ENS Lyon, 1994), Hornby & Pollack's L-system creature encoding (*Computers & Graphics* 25:1041–1048, 2001, doi:10.1016/S0097-8493(01)00157-1), and HyperNEAT/CPPN (Stanley, D'Ambrosio & Gauci, *Artificial Life* 15(2):185–212, 2009, doi:10.1162/artl.2009.15.2.15202). Your BRKGA-derived multi-segment int16 genome is, formally, a **fixed-length indirect encoding** that occupies a fourth, hybrid niche: the chromosome is fixed-shape but the decoder is generative (turtle-graphics forward kinematics), so the *phenotype* — number of branches, total piece count, switch placements — is variable. This places you alongside BRKGA, normalised-postfix VLSI floorplanning (the GA of Cohoon, Hegde, Martin & Richards style for slicing trees), and the indirect-encoding feature-selection NSGA-II of Saha et al. (the SBI initialisation work in *J. Comp. Design Eng.*) rather than alongside Koza-style GP.

**(2) Why standard crossover destroys construction sequences — and why your pilot's 70–95 % rate is exactly what theory predicts.** The destruction phenomenon you measured is the same effect that Poli & Langdon (*GPEM*, "Sub-tree Swapping Crossover, Allele Diffusion and GP Convergence," 2008) and O'Neill et al. (*GPEM* 4:67–93, 2003) call **context-sensitivity of position**: in a sequential decoder, the meaning of gene *i* depends on the partial state produced by genes *0…i−1*. A 1-point or uniform crossover that splices parent A's prefix with parent B's suffix breaks the precondition that B's suffix was evolved against — so the suffix's piece choices, switch flags, and branch slots no longer correspond to the geometric state the turtle has reached. This is the GP locality problem (Rothlauf & Oetzel, EuroGP 2006, doi:10.1007/11729976_29) and it is precisely why messy GAs use a **primordial phase** to discover good linkage groups before the juxtapositional cut-and-splice phase (Goldberg, Korb & Deb 1989), why Falkenauer's grouping GA operates on **groups rather than items** (*J. Heuristics* 2:5–30, 1996), why DSMGA-II (Hsu & Yu, GECCO 2015, doi:10.1145/2739480.2754737; arXiv:1807.11669) learns a Dependency Structure Matrix before mixing, and why hBOA (Pelikan & Goldberg) builds a Bayesian model of dependencies. The unifying lesson: **on construction-style genomes, recombination is destructive unless either (i) the encoding is canonicalised so positional meaning is parent-independent (BRKGA random keys, grouping GAs), or (ii) the operator is structurally aware (subtree crossover, group-preserving crossover, homologous crossover).** Langdon's size-fair and homologous crossover (*GPEM* 1(1/2):95–119, 2000, doi:10.1023/A:1010024515191) is the GP-side answer; BRKGA's segment-decoder pattern is the integer-key answer; both work because they restore the schema theorem's preconditions.

**(3) Sentinel/separator encodings and grouping GAs.** Your sentinel value S = n_piece_types is, in the taxonomy of Falkenauer (*Genetic Algorithms and Grouping Problems*, Wiley, 1998) and the VRP route-separator encodings surveyed by NEO/Universidad de Málaga, a **separator gene** that delimits sub-sequences without committing to their length. This is exactly the device used in capacitated VRP (vehicle identifiers as separators between routes), in multi-robot bridge-inspection routing (Harris et al., GECCO 2019, where k separator integers split an edge sequence into k routes), and in normalised-postfix VLSI slicing-tree GAs. The crucial property — preserved in your design — is that the *count* of sentinels in a chromosome can change under mutation while the chromosome *length* remains fixed, so NSGA-II's crowding distance, non-dominated sort, and binary tournament selection all operate on a uniform vector and need no modification. Falkenauer's key insight, which you should adopt explicitly: **mutation operators must respect the group/segment boundary and not blindly perturb across it.** Your BRANCH_MUTATE and FLIP_SWITCH_MASK already do this; SWAP_MAIN and REPLACE_MAIN must be guarded so they do not corrupt the sentinel positions or the switch-mask region.

**(4) NSGA-II machinery is essentially representation-agnostic when the genotype is fixed-length.** Deb et al.'s NSGA-II (*IEEE TEVC* 6(2):182–197, 2002, doi:10.1109/4235.996017) only requires (a) a dominance relation on objective vectors and (b) a crowding-distance estimate in objective space — neither depends on chromosome structure. The 2023 comprehensive survey by Verma, Pant & Snasel (*Artificial Intelligence Review* 56, doi:10.1007/s10462-023-10526-z) confirms that NSGA-II adaptations for variable-structure problems uniformly preserve the selection/sorting kernel and modify only sampling, crossover, and mutation. The Liu, Virgolin, Alderliesten & Bosman *evoNSGA-II* (arXiv:2202.06983, GECCO 2022) for symbolic-regression GP trees is the most directly relevant reference: they leave NSGA-II's sort untouched but add a **per-size survivor cap** to combat evolvability degeneration — small-tree over-replication. The analogous pathology on your problem would be **short-loop dominance** (small main-loop chromosomes that maximise piece utilisation trivially by using all small pieces), and the evoNSGA-II remedy — capping survivors per phenotypic-complexity bucket, e.g. per number-of-branches — is a directly transferable technique. The Cerf, Doerr et al. arXiv:2305.13459 paper provides the first proven runtime guarantee of NSGA-II on a combinatorial problem (bi-objective MST) and reinforces that NSGA-II's combinatorial behaviour is governed almost entirely by operator design, not by the sort.

**(5) Construction-based feasibility dominates repair-based feasibility for hard structural constraints.** The trade-off between (a) penalising/repairing infeasible solutions and (b) using a decoder that emits only feasible solutions has been studied repeatedly. Coello Coello's classic survey on constraint handling (*Comput. Methods Appl. Mech. Eng.* 191:1245–1287, 2002) and the Salcedo-Sanz survey on repair methods (*Comput. Sci. Rev.* 3:175–192, 2009, doi:10.1016/j.cosrev.2009.07.001) both note that for **hard structural constraints** — your closed-loop geometric constraint with switch-induced branching — repair operators tend to be (i) computationally as expensive as a decoder and (ii) statistically biased toward whatever local repair heuristic was chosen, distorting the search distribution. BRKGA's success across 250+ applications (Londe et al. 2025; Gonçalves & Resende 2011) is largely attributable to its construction-decoder discipline: the decoder *never* emits infeasible solutions, so the GA does not waste evaluations on penalty arithmetic and the Pareto front is populated entirely with feasible candidates. Your turtle-graphics forward-kinematics decoder is the correct architectural choice; the literature offers no example of a competitive evolutionary track/circuit/topology system that uses repair instead of construction once the geometric constraints are non-trivial.

**(6) Adaptive operator selection is the right pattern for branching-structure mutation portfolios.** Li, Fialho, Kwong & Zhang's FRRMAB (*IEEE TEVC* 18(1):114–130, 2014, doi:10.1109/TEVC.2013.2239648) is the canonical bandit-based AOS for MOEA/D and has been ported to NSGA-II in numerous follow-ups. Your ALNS-UCB1 hybrid scheduler with FRRMAB-style rank-based rewards is well-grounded: Hendel's α-UCB ALNS (*Math. Prog. Comp.* 14:185–221, 2022, doi:10.1007/s12532-021-00209-7) demonstrates that UCB-Tuned outperforms classical roulette-wheel weights on MIPLIB instances, and the broader ALNS literature (Pisinger & Ropke; Turkeš et al. 2021 meta-analysis showing only ~0.14 % improvement from classical adaptive weights) suggests that **bandit-based selection is meaningful only when the operator portfolio is genuinely diverse** — typically 5–8 operators, which matches your five mutation operators plus two crossover variants. The more recent DR-ALNS deep-RL approach (Reijnen et al., arXiv:2211.00759) outperforms FRRMAB on TSP variants but requires offline training; FRRMAB remains the best zero-shot choice and is what I recommend for your thesis.

## Details

**Variable-length GA foundations.** Goldberg, Korb & Deb's messy GA (*Complex Systems* 3:493–530, 1989) introduced the cut-and-splice operator and the over/underspecified chromosome — the original idea was that, since "nature did not start with strings of length 5.9 × 10⁹ and try to make man," evolution should grow chromosomes from short, well-tested building blocks. The fast messy GA (Goldberg, Deb, Kargupta & Harik, ICGA 1993) refined this with probabilistically complete initialisation. Empirically, messy GAs have been used for hardware evolution (Kajitani et al., VGA), node placement (the *Flexible GA* paper in *Information Sciences*, doi:10.1016/j.ins.2016.10.018), and topology optimisation (Kim & de Weck, *Struct. Multidiscip. Optim.* 29:445–456, 2005). The dominant lesson from this literature: **the cut-and-splice operator works only when the encoding has no positional context-sensitivity**, otherwise it suffers exactly the destruction problem you observed. This is why messy GAs work on deceptive concatenated trap functions but not on construction-style problems — and why the post-2015 variable-length GA literature has overwhelmingly shifted toward fixed-length indirect encodings (the BRKGA family, the *Sensors* 21:3990 sensor-scheduling VLC-GA which uses graph-based cut-and-splice on a directed-graph decoder rather than on the linear sequence directly).

**Tree representations and GP inside NSGA-II.** Koza's GP (1992) uses subtree crossover with depth/size limits; the bloat phenomenon — uncontrolled growth without fitness improvement — is its central pathology, analysed by Langdon & Poli (*Foundations of Genetic Programming*, Springer, 2002). For multi-objective variants, the dominant pattern in the last decade has been NSGA-II + GP tree with size as an explicit objective: Bleuler, Brack, Thiele & Zitzler ("Multiobjective genetic programming: reducing bloat using SPEA2," CEC 2001, doi:10.1109/CEC.2001.934430) showed that adding tree size as a second objective dominates classical parsimony-pressure penalties; this lineage culminates in evoNSGA-II (Liu et al., GECCO 2022, arXiv:2202.06983) which adds per-size evolvability tracking. Strongly Typed GP (Montana 1995) restricts subtree crossover to type-compatible nodes; Whigham's CFG-GP and O'Neill & Ryan's grammatical evolution (*IEEE TEVC* 5(4):349–358, 2001, doi:10.1109/4235.942529) generalise this through context-free grammars. Crossover in GE is famously fragile (O'Neill, Ryan, Keijzer & Cattolico, *GPEM* 4:67–93, 2003, doi:10.1023/A:1021877127167) — splicing integer codons across the genome can produce phenotypically wildly different programs because codon *i*'s meaning depends on which non-terminal the mapper has reached. **This is structurally identical to your problem** and the GE community's response — structured grammatical evolution (Lourenço, Pereira & Costa, *Handbook of Grammatical Evolution*, Springer, 2018, doi:10.1007/978-3-319-78717-6_6) which enforces a one-to-one genotype-non-terminal mapping — is essentially what your segmented int16 chromosome already does at the regional level: each region is dedicated to a specific phenotypic role, so crossover within a region is roughly homologous.

**Generative encodings for branching structures.** Gruau's cellular encoding (PhD, ENS Lyon, 1994; Whitley, Gruau & Pyeatt, ICGA 1995) evolves a tree of graph-rewriting cell-division rules and was the first scalable indirect encoding for branching neural topologies. Hornby & Pollack's L-system encoding for virtual creatures (*Computers & Graphics* 25:1041–1048, 2001, doi:10.1016/S0097-8493(01)00157-1) and their body-brain coevolution work demonstrated that L-systems generate creatures with hundreds of parts where direct encodings stall around 50; this is the most directly relevant precedent for a *generative* track-layout encoding because the L-system's `[…]` push/pop branch operators are formally the same as your switch-spawned sub-loops. HyperNEAT (Stanley, D'Ambrosio & Gauci, *Artificial Life* 15(2):185–212, 2009, doi:10.1162/artl.2009.15.2.15202) and the CPPN-based encodings achieve compactness via coordinate-conditioned weight functions but are most useful when the phenotype has strong geometric regularities — for a track layout where utilisation of a fixed inventory is one objective, regularity is helpful but not dominant, and the implementation overhead is high. **My recommendation against switching to a full L-system encoding for your thesis** is pragmatic: Hornby's work shows L-systems beat direct encodings, but BRKGA's record on 250+ combinatorial problems with simple integer-keyed segmented genomes (Londe et al., *EJOR* 321:1–22, 2025) is more comparable in problem class and far simpler to instrument with NSGA-II's diagnostic tools.

**Branching/multi-segment encodings.** Falkenauer's grouping GA (*Evol. Comput.* 2(2):123–144, 1994; *J. Heuristics* 2:5–30, 1996, doi:10.1007/BF00226291) introduced the two-part representation — items and groups — where crossover and mutation operate on the *group* level, preserving sub-structure. DSMGA-II (Hsu & Yu, GECCO 2015, doi:10.1145/2739480.2754737; arXiv:1807.11669) and the linkage-tree GA (Thierens & Bosman) learn linkage online and outperform hBOA on hierarchical trap problems; their relevance to your work is that **they justify segment-selective crossover empirically**: when linkage groups are known a priori (as they are in your design — the four chromosome regions), the per-region ρ vector is exactly the optimal-mixing pattern these algorithms try to discover automatically. Multi-population/co-evolutionary GAs (Potter & De Jong's CCGA, *PPSN* III, 1994; Fontes & Homayouni's bi-objective BRKGA in *EJOR* 2023) support a future extension where each branch evolves in a semi-independent sub-population.

**BRKGA and the per-region ρ pattern.** Gonçalves & Resende (*J. Heuristics* 17:487–525, 2011, doi:10.1007/s10732-010-9143-1) define BRKGA's parameterised uniform crossover with a single bias ρ ∈ (0.5, 1) toward the elite parent. The Multi-Parent BRKGA with Implicit Path-Relinking of Andrade, Toso, Gonçalves & Resende (*EJOR* 289(1):17–30, 2021, doi:10.1016/j.ejor.2019.11.037) generalises mating to multiple parents while keeping the per-key biased uniform crossover. Londe, Pessoa, Andrade & Resende's two reviews (*EJOR* 321:1–22, 2025, doi:10.1016/j.ejor.2024.03.030; *J. Global Optim.*, doi:10.1007/s10898-024-01446-5, 2025, "Early years…") survey 250+ applications and confirm that **a single global ρ, typically 0.7, is the standard**. Your design — ρ = {1.0, 0.7, 0.7, 0.7} where the main-loop region is inherited intact from the elite — is a principled extension corresponding to a **strongly biased main-loop schema with weakly biased peripheral structure**. The literature contains analogous *segmented BRKGAs* (Fontes & Homayouni, *EJOR* 306:1140–1157, 2023; Stefanello et al. 2019 on virtual-machine placement) where different decoder regions take different keys, and the Andrade-Toso multi-parent BRKGA's empirical results suggest your variant should be competitive. Multi-objective BRKGA hybridised with NSGA-II non-dominated sorting is established practice (Brandão, Noronha & Ribeiro 2016; the BRKGA-MP-IPR multi-objective variant, *EJOR* 2021).

**Mutation operator design for branching chromosomes.** GP's portfolio — subtree mutation, point mutation, hoist mutation, shrink mutation, permutation mutation (Poli, Langdon & McPhee, *A Field Guide to Genetic Programming*, lulu.com, 2008) — maps directly to your five-operator portfolio: SWAP_MAIN ↔ point mutation on the main sequence, REPLACE_MAIN ↔ point mutation with new symbol, FLIP_SWITCH_MASK ↔ structural mutation (analogous to architecture-altering operations of Koza's *GP III*), BRANCH_MUTATE ↔ subtree mutation, CROSSING_TOGGLE ↔ flag mutation. Field-Guide guidance ("Traditionally, 90 % of children are created by subtree crossover. However, the use of a 50-50 mixture of crossover and a variety of mutations also appears to work well") supports a **mutation-heavy regime** for problems where crossover is structurally fragile — which is your situation. The size-fair / homologous crossover of Langdon (*GPEM* 1:95–119, 2000, doi:10.1023/A:1010024515191) is the GP-side technique that biased per-region uniform crossover replicates in your design. Vanneschi's one-point-crossover-plus-subtree-mutation result (*Soft Computing* 16:171–184, 2012, doi:10.1007/s00500-010-0687-7) — practically eliminating bloat with a 50-50 crossover/mutation split — is a direct empirical justification for an AOS scheduler that maintains a mutation share around 0.5.

**Selection — leave it alone.** Binary tournament with rank + crowding distance (Deb et al. 2002) is robust to chromosome-internal structure because it operates only on objective vectors. The improved-crowding-distance literature (Chu & Yu, arXiv:1811.12667; Cerf, Doerr et al., arXiv:2305.13459 with theoretical guarantees on bi-objective MST) and the NSGA-II large-scale tuning study (López-Ibáñez et al. via *Math. Comput. Appl.* 27(6):103, 2022, doi:10.3390/mca27060103) confirm that auto-tuning of selection parameters via irace produces meaningful gains, but the kernel itself need not change for variable-phenotype-length problems. For your thesis, I recommend keeping NSGA-II's selection unmodified and reporting irace-tuned versus default configurations as a sensitivity study — this is the configuration that best matches the comparable NAS literature (NSGA-Net of Lu, Whalen, Boddeti, Dhebar, Deb, Goodman & Banzhaf, arXiv:1810.03522, GECCO 2019, doi:10.1145/3321707.3321729) where NSGA-II is used out-of-the-box on a complex graph-structured search space.

**Crowding distance with phenotype-aware augmentation.** A subtle issue is that NSGA-II's crowding distance is computed in objective space, so two solutions with very different topologies (one with 0 branches, one with 5) but similar (f₁, f₂) values are treated as crowded. For variable-structure problems this can lead to phenotypic monoculture — a known issue in MOGP (Bleuler et al. 2001; the *evolvability degeneration* result of Liu, Virgolin et al., arXiv:2202.06983). Two practical remedies are documented: (i) **add structural diversity as an objective** (as in MOGP with tree size; equivalent for you would be number of branches or topological signature), making it a 3-objective NSGA-II problem; or (ii) **per-bucket survivor caps** as in evoNSGA-II — limit the fraction of the next-generation population that may have any given branch count. Both are cheap to implement. I recommend the latter for your thesis because it preserves the natural f₁/f₂ formulation.

**Domain analogues.** Direct precedent for a track/route/topology problem with NSGA-II is sparse. Railway scheduling with permutation-based GA + greedy decoder (Juillé; "On the Benefits of Inoculation," arXiv:cs/0611140) follows the same construction-decoder pattern. The covering salesman problem with variable-length 1-D arrays inside NSGA-II (Singh et al., *Neural Comput. Appl.* 35, doi:10.1007/s00521-022-07683-7) is the closest direct example of NSGA-II on a variable-tour problem. Hornby & Pollack's L-system creature work (above) is the closest precedent for branching structure design. **No published GA/EA work on LEGO/4DBrix-compatible track layout with NSGA-II appears to exist** — this is a genuine gap your thesis fills, and it should be framed as such rather than as an incremental contribution to an existing line. The closest neighbours are BlueBrick (Nanty) and 4DBrix nControl, both interactive design tools without optimisation, and the modular-robot reconfiguration literature (Pasumarthi et al., *Sci. Rep.* 14, 2025, doi:10.1038/s41598-024-84637-0) which uses multi-objective GA for assembly position rather than topology.

## Recommendations

**Stage 1 — Keep your current architecture and instrument it.** Your fixed-length multi-segment int16 chromosome with the sentinel S, four-region biased crossover with ρ = {1.0, 0.7, 0.7, 0.7}, five typed mutation operators, and turtle-graphics decoder is defensible against the entire literature reviewed above. It corresponds to a multi-segment BRKGA with structurally typed mutation — a configuration that the *EJOR* 2025 BRKGA review cites as best-practice for problems where the decoder reflects domain structure. Before changing anything, instrument the per-operator improvement rate over 50–100 generations on a fixed instance set; this is the FRRMAB reward signal and also the diagnostic that will tell you which operators are pulling weight.

**Stage 2 — Add a phenotypic-diversity safeguard.** Implement evoNSGA-II-style per-bucket survivor caps (Liu, Virgolin, Alderliesten & Bosman, GECCO 2022, arXiv:2202.06983) keyed on the number of branches in the decoded phenotype. Cap the fraction of the population that may share a branch count at, e.g., 0.3. Threshold to revisit: if hypervolume plateaus before generation 200 and Pareto-front samples cluster at one or two branch counts, raise the cap or switch to (Stage 3).

**Stage 3 — If Stage 2 plateaus, add branch count as a third objective.** Adding |branches| as f₃ to be either maximised or held in a target range converts the problem to 3-objective NSGA-II. This is the MOGP-bloat solution applied to your domain (Bleuler et al. 2001) and is empirically more robust than single-objective parsimony pressure. NSGA-II's many-objective pathologies start to bite at four or more objectives (Cerf et al., arXiv:2305.13459); three is safe.

**Stage 4 — Tune the AOS portfolio.** Run FRRMAB (Li, Fialho, Kwong & Zhang, *IEEE TEVC* 18(1):114–130, 2014, doi:10.1109/TEVC.2013.2239648) with sliding-window size W = 50, decay parameter D = 1.0, and rank scaling factor C = 5.0 — these are the original paper's defaults. Compare against α-UCB (Hendel, *Math. Prog. Comp.* 14:185–221, 2022, doi:10.1007/s12532-021-00209-7) on identical seeds. Report which mutations dominate at early/middle/late generations — the hypothesis from analogous problems is that BRANCH_MUTATE dominates early (structural exploration), SWAP_MAIN/REPLACE_MAIN dominate middle (refinement), CROSSING_TOGGLE dominates late (local optimisation).

**Stage 5 — Only if Stages 1-4 underperform: switch encoding.** Move to an explicit tree genome (Strongly Typed GP per Montana 1995) or a generative L-system (Hornby & Pollack 2001) **only** if the BRKGA-style approach demonstrably plateaus below acceptable hypervolume on instances where a manual baseline is known to be feasible. This is a thesis chapter's worth of work and almost certainly unnecessary; it is included for completeness. The benchmark to trigger Stage 5 is: hypervolume gap > 20 % versus a hand-designed reference layout after 1000 generations with population 200.

## Caveats

- **The 70–95 % destruction rate is reported as your pilot finding.** The literature predicts this magnitude qualitatively (Poli, Langdon & McPhee 2008; O'Neill et al. 2003) but does not give a precise quantitative benchmark for your specific encoding; treat the number as motivating evidence, not as a literature-confirmed datapoint, in the thesis.
- **No prior work on LEGO/4DBrix track layout with EA was found.** I performed multiple targeted searches (LEGO + GA, 4DBrix + algorithm, BlueBrick + optimisation) and the only adjacent results are interactive CAD tools (BlueBrick, 4DBrix nControl, 3D Train Studio) and unrelated GA work on full-scale railway maintenance/scheduling. The thesis should explicitly position itself as the first such work and frame this as a contribution rather than a literature gap to be apologised for.
- **BRKGA's multi-objective extensions are still maturing.** Fontes & Homayouni (*EJOR* 306, 2023) report a multi-objective BRKGA outperforming NSGA-II on bi-objective energy-efficient job-shop with transport — but the broader BRKGA literature (Londe et al., *EJOR* 321:1–22, 2025) notes multi-objective BRKGA is still under-studied. Your hybrid (BRKGA-style decoder with NSGA-II's selection kernel) is therefore on the methodological frontier; this is good for novelty but means you should validate your operator portfolio carefully against simpler baselines (single-objective BRKGA per objective; vanilla NSGA-II with random integer crossover) and report those baselines.
- **FRRMAB's effectiveness depends on operator diversity.** Turkeš, Sörensen & Hvattum's meta-analysis of ALNS (cited in the *Math. Prog. Comp.* 14:185–221, 2022 paper) found classical adaptive weights yield only ~0.14 % average improvement on operator-poor portfolios. With your five mutations + two crossover variants the portfolio is borderline; if FRRMAB shows no advantage over uniform random selection, the portfolio is too small or too redundant rather than the AOS being broken.
- **NSGA-II's crowding distance is increasingly questioned for many-objective settings** (Cerf, Doerr et al., arXiv:2305.13459; the new "truthful" crowding distance of arXiv:2407.17687 with provable guarantees). For your bi-objective problem this is not an issue, but if you extend to three or four objectives, NSGA-III (Deb & Jain, *IEEE TEVC* 18(4):577–601, 2014, doi:10.1109/TEVC.2013.2281535) or the truthful-crowding NSGA-II variant should be considered.
- **L-system / generative encodings remain an under-explored frontier for this specific problem.** Hornby & Pollack 2001 demonstrated dramatic scaling advantages of L-systems over direct encodings for branching morphologies, and a future-work direction is whether an L-system that emits piece-placement turtle commands could outperform your segmented integer genome at large inventory sizes. I deliberately do *not* recommend this for the thesis itself — the implementation cost is high and the comparison is fragile — but it is the strongest open research question this work raises.
- **Adaptive operator selection literature is moving toward deep RL** (DR-ALNS, arXiv:2211.00759; AdaW-DDQN in *Neurocomputing* 2024, doi:10.1016/j.neucom.2024.127391). For a thesis-scale project, FRRMAB is the right choice; flag DR-ALNS as future work rather than committing to it.
